How Long Should a Blog Post Be for SEO in 2026?

By [YOUR NAME] · Published November 13, 2026 · 8 min read

"How long should a blog post be?" is one of the most searched questions in content marketing, and the answers online range from 600 words to 3,000 or more. Both camps have data to point to, which is why the question stays confusing. This guide sorts out what the research actually shows, why longer isn't automatically better, how AI-powered search is changing the picture in 2026, and how to find the right length for your own topic.

The short answer

For a competitive informational topic, most ranking posts land between about 1,500 and 2,500 words. But there is no magic number, and Google has said publicly that word count is not a ranking factor. The right length is however many words it takes to fully answer what the searcher wants, and no more. Sometimes that is 700 words, and sometimes it is 3,500.

What the studies show, and what they don't

A widely cited analysis from Backlinko, which looked at millions of Google results, found that pages on the first page of search averaged roughly 1,450 words. HubSpot examined its own most-read blog posts and found they ran considerably longer, at around 2,300 words on average, with how-to posts landing nearer 1,600 to 2,000. Other studies report that long-form posts attract more backlinks than short ones.

These are correlations, not instructions. Pages that rank well tend to be thorough, and thorough pages tend to be long. But adding 800 words of filler to a thin article doesn't make it thorough. The likely cause of the ranking is topical completeness, which length only reflects. If you treat the average as a target, you risk padding, which hurts readers and rarely helps rankings.

Word count by content type

Different content has different jobs. These ranges are rough editorial guidelines drawn from common practice, not hard rules:

Content typeTypical lengthGoal
News update or short announcement300–600 wordsQuick, timely answer
Standard blog post or opinion piece800–1,500 wordsOne idea, well explained
How-to guide or tutorial1,500–2,000 wordsComplete step-by-step help
Ultimate guide or pillar page2,500–4,000 wordsCover a broad topic in depth
Original data study or research1,500–3,500 wordsPresent findings and methods
Product or category page description150–400 wordsHelp a buyer decide

Why depth matters more than length

Search engines try to match each query to the page that best satisfies it. A good page anticipates the follow-up questions a reader will have, covers the main subtopics, gives concrete examples, and is easy to scan. That tends to take space, so thorough pages are often long. But a tightly written 900-word post that answers the question directly can outrank a rambling 3,000-word one.

Readers behave the same way. People leave when they can't find the answer, and an inflated introduction or repeated points push the answer down the page. If a section doesn't add something new, cut it. Our guide to cutting a draft to a strict word limit covers practical ways to do that.

Writing for AI search in 2026

AI-generated answers, such as AI Overviews in Google, now sit above many results. Ahrefs analyzed hundreds of thousands of AI Overviews and the pages they cite, and the takeaway is that stretching a post past 2,500 words doesn't improve your chance of being cited. What matters is whether a passage clearly answers the specific question being asked.

That suggests a few habits that work for both readers and AI systems:

  • Answer first. Put a clear, direct answer in the first sentence or two under each heading, then add detail afterward.
  • Use question-style headings. A heading like "How long should a how-to guide be?" matches how people search and how AI tools pull out answers.
  • Write self-contained passages. A paragraph of 40 to 80 words that makes sense on its own is easy to quote and easy to skim.
  • Use tables and lists where they fit. Comparisons and step sequences are clearer in structured form.
  • Show real expertise. Original examples, first-hand details, and named sources make your page more trustworthy than a generic summary.

How to find the right length for your keyword

The most reliable method is to look at what already ranks for your target search. Here is a simple process using the free WordCounterPro editor:

  1. Search your target keyword and open the top five or six results.
  2. Copy each article's main text into the counter, without the menus, comments, or footers, and note the word count.
  3. Look at the range. If competitors run 900 to 1,300 words, a 2,500-word post is probably overkill. If they run 2,000 to 3,000, a 700-word post likely won't compete.
  4. List what they cover. Note the subtopics, questions, and examples across the top results, and find gaps they all miss.
  5. Write to be complete, not to hit a number. Cover the full list, add something original, and stop when the reader's question is answered.

Use the keyword density and readability panels in the editor to check the result. Our guide to avoiding keyword stuffing in modern SEO explains why natural wording beats repetition, and understanding Flesch–Kincaid readability helps you pitch the reading level to your audience.

Padding traps to avoid

  • The long wind-up. Skip the history of the internet and get to the point within the first few paragraphs.
  • Repeating yourself. Restating a point in different words adds length without value.
  • Generic filler. Sentences that could appear in any article on any topic dilute yours.
  • Keyword stuffing. Forcing a phrase into every paragraph reads badly and can hurt you.
  • Splitting one topic across many thin pages. One thorough page usually beats five shallow ones.

A quick checklist before you publish

  1. Does the opening answer the main question within the first 100 words?
  2. Does every section add a new idea, example, or step?
  3. Is your length in line with what the top results for this keyword offer?
  4. Are the headings clear, and are the answers under them self-contained?
  5. Did you include something original, such as an example, a number, or a first-hand detail?
  6. Does the page read well aloud? Use the Read Aloud feature to find clumsy spots.

Frequently asked questions

Is 500 words enough for a blog post?

It can be, for a narrow question or a news update. For competitive informational searches, 500 words rarely covers the topic well enough to compete with longer, more complete pages.

Is 2,000 words too long?

Not if every section earns its place. Two thousand words is common for how-to guides. It becomes too long when it repeats itself or buries the answer.

Does Google rank longer posts higher?

Google has said word count is not a ranking factor. Longer posts often rank well because they tend to be more complete, but length alone doesn't help.

How many words should the introduction be?

Usually 50 to 150 words. State what the reader will learn and, where possible, give the short answer right away.

Should I update old posts or write new ones?

If an older post ranks but has slipped, updating it with fresher information, a clearer structure, and better answers is often faster and more effective than starting over.
