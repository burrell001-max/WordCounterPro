Using Flow Score to Fix Monotone Writing

By Kent Williams · Published November 13, 2026 · 8 min read

Some writing is technically correct and still tiring to read. The grammar is fine, the words are clear, but something about it feels flat, like a drum playing one beat. Very often the cause is sentence rhythm: too many sentences of about the same length in a row. This guide explains what rhythm is, how the Flow Score in WordCounterPro measures it, what the numbers mean, and how to fix a draft that sounds monotone.

What is sentence rhythm?

Rhythm is the pattern of long and short sentences in a piece of writing. Even when we read silently, we hear it. A short sentence lands like a punch. A long one, with several clauses that build and turn before it finally arrives at its point, carries the reader along. Good writers mix them, usually without noticing.

Monotone writing happens when that mix disappears. Five sentences of about twelve words each feel mechanical, and so do five very short ones in a row, which sound choppy, or five long ones, which wear the reader out. Readability formulas such as Flesch–Kincaid look at average sentence length, so they can't tell a varied paragraph from a monotone one with the same average. Flow Score was built to fill that gap.

How Flow Score works

When you turn on Flow Score in the editor, it does three things:

  1. Sorts every sentence into one of six length categories: very short (1–4 words), short (5–9), medium (10–17), long (18–25), very long (26–34), and extra long (35 or more). Each category gets its own color in the preview, so you can see the pattern at a glance.
  2. Flags repeated runs. If three or more sentences in a row fall in the same category, all of them are marked as a repeated pattern.
  3. Calculates a score from 0 to 100. It measures how much your sentence lengths vary compared with their average (the standard deviation divided by the mean, scaled up), then subtracts a penalty based on how much of your text sits inside flagged runs.

The score needs at least three sentences. The result is labeled Excellent variety at 70 or above, Fairly even rhythm from 40 to 69, and Repetitive rhythm below 40.

A worked example

Here is the same scene written two ways. First, a monotone version:

The park opens at eight o'clock every morning during the summer. Families arrive early and look for a shady spot near the lake. Children run toward the playground while their parents unpack the picnic baskets. Dogs chase each other across the grass and splash in the shallow water. By noon the whole park is crowded with people enjoying the warm sunshine. The crowd slowly thins out as the afternoon heat becomes too strong.

Now the same content with the rhythm rebuilt:

The park opens at eight. By nine, families have claimed every shady spot near the lake, spread out their blankets, and unpacked enough food to feed twice their number. Children sprint for the playground. Dogs chase each other across the grass. Then, around noon, the whole place fills with people soaking in the warm sunshine, laughing, napping, and arguing cheerfully over whose turn it is to hold the umbrella. Quiet returns by evening.

How to fix a low Flow Score

  1. Break up the longest run first. Find a group of flagged sentences and change one of them. Splitting a medium sentence into two short ones, or joining two into one long one, usually fixes the run.
  2. Follow a long sentence with a short one. A brief sentence after a complicated one gives the reader a place to rest and emphasizes your point.
  3. Combine choppy sentences. Several very short sentences in a row can usually be joined with "and," "but," "because," or a comma.
  4. Vary how sentences begin. If three in a row start with "The" or "I," rewriting the opener often changes the length naturally.
  5. Read the result aloud. The Read Aloud button in the editor lets you hear whether the new rhythm sounds natural or forced.

When a low score is fine

Flow Score is a diagnostic tool, not a grade. A low score doesn't always mean bad writing:

  • Deliberate repetition. Speeches, poetry and slogans often repeat a pattern on purpose for emphasis.
  • Technical or legal writing. Instructions and specifications favor consistency over style.
  • Very short passages. With only a few sentences, the score can swing sharply from one edit.
  • Lists and headings. These aren't sentences in the usual sense and can distort the measurement.

A high score doesn't guarantee good writing either. You can vary sentence lengths randomly and still write something unclear. Use the score to spot patterns, then trust your ear.

Flow Score and readability together

The two measures answer different questions. Reading level asks how hard your sentences are on average. Flow Score asks how varied they are. Short sentences lower the grade level, but too many in a row will drag the Flow Score down. Aim for a reading level suited to your audience, as explained in our guide to Flesch–Kincaid readability, and enough variety that the text keeps moving.

The same applies if you read your work aloud. Our guide to speech writing, timing and word counts covers writing for the ear, where rhythm matters even more. And if you are shortening a draft, cutting to a strict word limit is a good moment to recheck your rhythm, since heavy cuts can leave behind a run of similar sentences.

Try it on your own writing

  1. Open the WordCounterPro editor and paste in at least three sentences.
  2. Turn on the Flow Score switch in the sidebar.
  3. Look at the color pattern in the preview. Long stretches of one color, or flagged runs, show where to edit.
  4. Revise, and watch the score update as you type.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good Flow Score?

In WordCounterPro, 70 or above is labeled excellent variety, and 40 to 69 is a fairly even rhythm. Below 40 suggests a repetitive pattern worth reviewing.

Why does my score say "Write at least 3 sentences"?

Measuring variety needs a few data points. With fewer than three sentences, the tool has nothing meaningful to compare.

Does Flow Score measure grammar or quality?

No. It only looks at sentence length and patterns. It can't tell whether your sentences are accurate, clear, or well argued.

Can the score reach 100?

Yes, the score is capped at 100. Text with a wide mix of very short and very long sentences, and no repeated runs, can reach it, though that mix isn't always what you want.

Are fragments and one-word sentences okay?

In moderation, yes. A short fragment can add emphasis, and Flow Score counts it as a very short sentence. Too many in a row, though, will be flagged as a repeated pattern.
