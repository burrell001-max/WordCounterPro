Speech Writing: Timing and Word Counts

By Kent Williams · Published November 13, 2026 · 8 min read

"How long should my speech be?" almost always turns into "how many words is that?" Whether you are writing a three-minute wedding toast, a ten-minute class presentation, or a twenty-minute conference talk, the number of words you write decides how long you will speak. Get it wrong and you will either rush through the end or run out of things to say. This guide gives you the numbers, shows you the simple math behind them, and walks through a process for timing and trimming your draft.

The quick answer: about 150 words per minute

A comfortable speaking pace for most people is somewhere between 130 and 160 words per minute. For planning, 150 words per minute is a good middle figure, and it is the default speaking speed in the WordCounterPro editor. At that pace, a three-minute speech is about 450 words and a ten-minute speech is about 1,500 words.

Your own pace may differ. Nervous speakers often rush, while speakers who use long pauses and slow, deliberate delivery may run closer to 110 or 120. Treat the numbers below as a starting point, then time yourself reading aloud.

The formula is just words ÷ words-per-minute = minutes. So a draft of 1,800 words at 150 words per minute takes 12 minutes to deliver.

Why the math is only the starting point

A word count tells you how long the words take, but a real speech includes more than words. Several things add time:

  • Pauses. A deliberate pause before a key point, or after a joke, can take two or three seconds each. Ten of them add up to half a minute.
  • Audience reactions. Laughter, applause and nodding all take time, especially at weddings, award ceremonies and keynotes.
  • Slides and demos. Switching slides, running a demo, or showing a video takes time that doesn't appear in your script.
  • Stories and asides. Spoken delivery tends to wander a little, even when you are well rehearsed.

As a rule of thumb, plan for about 10 percent fewer words than the math suggests. For a ten-minute slot, aim for roughly 1,350 words rather than 1,500.

Word counts for common occasions

These are typical lengths that audiences find comfortable. Always follow the organizer's instructions when you have them.

  • Wedding toast: 2 to 3 minutes, about 300 to 450 words. Short toasts are remembered more kindly than long ones.
  • Introduction of a speaker: 1 to 2 minutes, about 150 to 300 words.
  • Class presentation: 5 to 10 minutes, about 750 to 1,500 words, depending on the assignment.
  • Eulogy: commonly 5 to 10 minutes, about 750 to 1,500 words. Ask the family or officiant what they prefer.
  • Business pitch: 5 to 10 minutes, about 750 to 1,500 words, leaving time for questions.
  • Conference talk: 20 to 45 minutes, about 3,000 to 6,750 words, but these are often delivered from slides and notes rather than a full script.

TED recommends that talks stay under 18 minutes, which is around 2,700 words at an average pace, and many presenters find shorter is better.

How to time your speech accurately

  1. Draft first, count second. Paste your speech into the WordCounterPro editor and check the speaking-time estimate in the details panel. You can set your own words-per-minute in Settings.
  2. Read it aloud with a timer. Speak at the pace you plan to use, not as fast as possible. Use the Read Aloud feature to hear the rhythm of the text.
  3. Calculate your real pace. Divide your word count by the minutes you took. If you finished 450 words in 3 minutes 20 seconds, your pace is about 135 words per minute. Enter that in Settings for a more accurate estimate next time.
  4. Add the extras. Include the time for pauses, slides, and audience reaction.
  5. Rehearse at least twice more. Run times usually shrink after the first read-through as you get more fluent, so check the final length once you are comfortable with the material.

Trimming a speech that runs long

Suppose your draft is 1,800 words but your slot is 10 minutes, which is about 1,500 words at 150 words per minute. You need to cut about 300 words, which is roughly 17 percent. The same principles that apply to written cutting work here, but with a few spoken-word twists:

  • Cut whole ideas before whole words. Listeners can only follow so many points. Three strong points beat six rushed ones.
  • Keep your best story, cut the rest. One vivid example is more memorable than several quick ones.
  • Don't trim the opening and closing. Audiences remember the first and last minute most, so cut from the middle instead.
  • Remove anything that reads well but sounds awkward. Long, nested sentences are hard to follow by ear.

Writing for the ear, not the eye

A speech that reads beautifully on the page can sound stiff out loud. People hear it once, at your pace, and can't re-read a confusing sentence. A few habits help:

  • Use shorter sentences. Aim for an easy reading level. The Flesch–Kincaid guide explains how to check it, and a grade 6 to 8 level is a good target for most audiences.
  • Vary your rhythm. A run of same-length sentences sounds monotone when spoken. See using Flow Score to fix monotone writing.
  • Repeat key ideas on purpose. Repetition that would feel redundant in an essay helps listeners follow along.
  • Use signposts. Phrases like "first," "the second reason," and "to wrap up" tell the audience where they are.
  • Write the way you talk. Contractions are fine, and so are occasional sentence fragments.

Frequently asked questions

How many words is a 5-minute speech?

About 750 words at an average pace of 150 words per minute, or roughly 650 to 850 at slower and faster paces.

How many pages is that?

A double-spaced page in a 12-point font holds about 250 to 300 words, so a 750-word speech runs about three pages. Many speakers use a larger font so the script is easier to read at a glance.

Is it better to memorize or read a speech?

Both work, but most speakers do best somewhere in between: know the structure and key lines by heart, and keep notes or a script for the details. Reading a full script word for word tends to sound flat unless you practice a lot.

How slowly should I speak?

Slow enough that the back of the room can follow, with pauses at the end of key thoughts. Many nervous speakers speed up without noticing, so deliberately aim a little slower than feels natural.

What if I finish early?

That is much better than going over. Audiences rarely complain about a speech that ends a little short, but they notice quickly when one drags on.
