Cutting a Draft to a Strict Word Limit

By [YOUR NAME] · Published November 13, 2026 · 7 min read

Every writer hits the same wall sooner or later: the draft is 1,140 words, and the limit is 900. College application essays, scholarship statements, journal abstracts, grant summaries, and conference proposals all enforce hard caps, and a form that stops accepting text at the limit can't be argued with. The good news is that most drafts can lose 15 to 25 percent of their length and come out stronger. This guide walks through a repeatable method for doing it.

1. Find out exactly how the limit is counted

Before you cut anything, confirm the rules. Some organizations count every word, including headings and citations. Others exclude the reference list, footnotes, or the title. Some limits are in characters, with or without spaces, rather than words. A hyphenated phrase like "well-known" is one word in some counters and two in others, and an em dash with no spaces around it can glue two words into one.

Paste your draft into a counter and compare the result with the number your target system reports. If they disagree, trust the target system. Then set yourself a working goal about 2 to 3 percent under the limit, because a late edit or a different counting method can easily push you over.

2. Work from big cuts to small cuts

The most common mistake is to start by deleting adverbs one at a time. That produces a draft that is only a few words shorter and often reads worse. Cut in layers instead, starting with the largest units:

  1. Sections and paragraphs. Ask what the piece would lose if a whole paragraph disappeared. If the honest answer is "not much," it can go. One paragraph is often 100 words or more.
  2. Examples and anecdotes. Keep the strongest example for each point and drop the rest. Two examples that make the same point cost twice as much as one.
  3. Sentences. Remove sentences that repeat the previous one in different words, announce what you're about to say, or restate what you just said.
  4. Phrases and words. Only after the structure is settled should you tighten wording. Trimming a paragraph you later delete is wasted effort.

3. Set a word budget for each section

Numbers keep you honest. Count the words in each section, then decide where they matter most. A 900-word application essay might look like this:

  • Opening hook and context: about 120 words
  • Main story or argument: about 450 words
  • Reflection on what you learned: about 220 words
  • Closing: about 80 words

When one section runs over, you can see immediately which part of the piece needs the cut instead of shaving every paragraph evenly. The WordCounterPro editor shows live counts as you type, so you can watch a section shrink toward its budget.

4. Cut the common word-wasters

Once the structure is right, these patterns account for most of the easy savings:

  • Wordy openers. "In order to" becomes "to," "due to the fact that" becomes "because," and "at this point in time" becomes "now."
  • Throat-clearing. Phrases like "it is important to note that" and "it goes without saying" can almost always be deleted outright.
  • Stacked qualifiers. "Very," "really," "quite," and "somewhat" rarely add information. If a sentence needs three of them, the verb or adjective is probably too weak.
  • Noun forms of verbs. "Make a determination" is "determine," and "conduct an investigation of" is "investigate." Turning these back into verbs shortens sentences and makes them livelier.
  • Passive constructions. "The report was written by the committee" is "The committee wrote the report," which saves two words and names who did the work.
  • Redundant pairs. "Each and every," "first and foremost," and "plan ahead" all say the same thing twice.

A worked example

Here are three sentences before and after tightening. Meaning is unchanged, but the word counts drop sharply.

Before (43 words): In order to be able to make a determination about whether or not the proposal is feasible, it is important that we take into consideration the fact that the budget has already been significantly reduced due to the fact that funding was cut.

After (20 words): To decide whether the proposal is feasible, we must consider that the budget is already smaller because funding was cut.

Before (22 words): There are many students who find that they struggle with the process of writing a conclusion that is both short and effective.

After (9 words): Many students struggle to write a short, effective conclusion.

Before (23 words): The results of the study were very clearly showing that the new method was really quite a lot faster than the old one.

After (14 words): The study clearly showed the new method was much faster than the old one.

Across just these three sentences the total falls from 88 words to 43, a cut of about 51 percent. Apply that to a whole draft and a 1,140-word essay lands comfortably under 900.

5. Protect what the reader needs

Cutting is not the same as flattening. Before you delete a detail, ask whether it is the thing that makes your piece specific. A concrete image, a number, or a short line of dialogue often does more work than a general statement three times its length. Keep the details that only you could have written and cut the generic ones that anyone could.

Also watch your rhythm. A run of very short sentences can feel choppy, and tightening everything to the same length makes prose monotone. Our guide to using Flow Score to fix monotone writing explains how to check for that after a heavy edit.

6. Do a final pass

  1. Read the piece aloud, or use the Read Aloud feature in the editor. Places where you stumble are usually places where a cut left a gap.
  2. Check that transitions still connect. Deleting a sentence can strand the one after it, so a short bridge may need to go back in.
  3. Recount using the same method as the target system, and confirm you are under the limit.
  4. Step away for an hour or a day, then read it once more. Fresh eyes often find five or ten more words to trim, or catch an accidental error.

Frequently asked questions

How much can I realistically cut without hurting the piece?

Most first drafts can lose 15 to 25 percent. Beyond that, you usually need to remove ideas, not just words, so decide which point matters least and cut it entirely.

Should I write long and cut, or write to the limit from the start?

Both work. Writing long and cutting tends to produce tighter, more confident prose, while writing to the limit forces early decisions. Pick whichever keeps you moving.

Do citations and headings count toward the limit?

It depends on the organization. Always check the written instructions, and when they are unclear, ask the organizer. Assuming wrong is a common reason for rejected submissions.
