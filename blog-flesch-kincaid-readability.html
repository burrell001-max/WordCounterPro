Understanding Flesch–Kincaid Readability

By Kent Williams · Published November 13, 2026 · 8 min read

You have probably seen a readability score at the bottom of a document editor, a plugin, or a word counter and wondered what the number actually means. Most of those scores trace back to the work of Rudolf Flesch in the 1940s and J. Peter Kincaid in the 1970s. The formulas are simple, and knowing how they work helps you use them well, and also helps you see where they mislead. This guide explains both Flesch–Kincaid measures, shows a worked example, and gives practical ways to improve your score.

What Flesch–Kincaid measures

There are really two related tests. Both look at the same two ingredients: how long your sentences are, and how long your words are, measured in syllables.

  • Flesch Reading Ease gives a score from roughly 0 to 100, where higher means easier. It was developed first.
  • Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level converts the same ingredients into a U.S. school grade. A score of 8.0 means the text should be understandable to a typical eighth-grader. Kincaid and colleagues created it for the U.S. Navy to check the difficulty of technical manuals.

WordCounterPro's reading level indicator uses the Grade Level formula.

The formulas

Here are the two formulas in plain form:

Grade Level = 0.39 × (words ÷ sentences) + 11.8 × (syllables ÷ words) − 15.59

Reading Ease = 206.835 − 1.015 × (words ÷ sentences) − 84.6 × (syllables ÷ words)

The pattern is easy to see. Longer sentences and longer words both push the Grade Level up and the Reading Ease down. Nothing else about your writing enters the calculation.

A worked example

Suppose a passage has 100 words, 5 sentences, and 140 syllables in total.

  • Average sentence length is 100 ÷ 5 = 20 words.
  • Average syllables per word is 140 ÷ 100 = 1.4.
  • Grade Level = 0.39 × 20 + 11.8 × 1.4 − 15.59 = 8.7, or about grade 9.
  • Reading Ease = 206.835 − 1.015 × 20 − 84.6 × 1.4 = 68, which falls in the "plain English" range.

What grade level should you aim for?

It depends on your audience and purpose. There is no single right answer, but these are common targets:

  • General web content and blog posts: about grades 7 to 9. Most readers skim on a phone, and easier text is faster to take in.
  • Public health, government and customer instructions: often grades 6 to 8, because the audience is broad and the stakes are high.
  • Academic papers and technical reports: often grades 12 and above, since readers expect specialized vocabulary.
  • Fiction: varies widely. Many popular novels score between grades 4 and 8.

A lower score is not automatically better. If your readers are specialists, forcing a sixth-grade style can make the writing feel childish or imprecise.

The limits of the formula

Flesch–Kincaid is a quick signal, not a verdict on quality. Keep these limits in mind:

  • It ignores meaning. A nonsense sentence made of short words scores as very easy to read.
  • Short words can still be hard. Terms like "quark," "tort," or "yield" are brief but unfamiliar to many readers.
  • Long words can be easy. "Information," "television," and "family" are polysyllabic but familiar.
  • Syllable counting is approximate. Software guesses, so scores differ slightly between tools.
  • It doesn't see structure. Headings, lists, examples and clear organization matter a great deal to real readers but not to the formula.
  • Very short texts are unreliable. A few sentences can swing wildly. The score is steadier on at least 100 words.

How to lower your grade level

  1. Split long sentences. This usually has the biggest effect. Look for sentences with several commas, "which" clauses, or stacked ideas.
  2. Swap long words for plain ones. "Use" instead of "utilize," "help" instead of "facilitate," "start" instead of "commence."
  3. Prefer active voice. "The committee approved the plan" is shorter and clearer than "The plan was approved by the committee."
  4. Cut filler phrases. Our guide to cutting a draft to a strict word limit covers the common offenders.
  5. Break up big paragraphs. The formula doesn't measure this, but readers certainly notice.

Before and after

Here is one long sentence rewritten as three. The meaning stays the same:

Before (45 words, 1 sentence): Although the committee had initially expressed considerable reservations regarding the proposed restructuring of the department, which many members believed would negatively affect employee morale and productivity, it ultimately approved the plan after receiving assurances from management that additional support would be provided during the transition.

After (32 words, 3 sentences): The committee first had serious doubts about restructuring the department. Many members feared it would hurt morale and productivity. Management promised extra support during the change, so the committee approved the plan.

The text got shorter, and the average sentence length fell from 45 words to about 11. Under the formula, that change alone lowers the Grade Level by roughly 13 points (0.39 × 45 versus 0.39 × 11), and the simpler words ("doubts" for "reservations," "hurt" for "negatively affect") lower it further.

Readability and SEO

Search engines don't publish a requirement for a particular Flesch–Kincaid score, and it is not known to be a direct ranking factor. Still, readable pages tend to keep visitors longer and are easier to share, which supports the signals that do matter. Aim for the level your audience needs, then check it. Frequently asked questions

What is a good Flesch–Kincaid score?

For general audiences, a Grade Level of 7 to 9 (or a Reading Ease of 60 to 70) is a common target. The best score depends on who is reading.

Is Flesch Reading Ease the same as Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level?

They use the same inputs but report them differently. Reading Ease is a 0–100 score where higher is easier, and Grade Level is a U.S. school grade where higher is harder.

Why do different tools give different scores?

They count syllables and detect sentence endings in slightly different ways. Abbreviations, numbers and bullet lists can all shift the result.

Can the grade level be negative or very high?

Yes. Very short sentences of one-syllable words can produce a score below zero, and long, dense sentences can push it past 20. Extreme scores usually mean the sample is unusual or too short.

Does a lower grade level mean better writing?

No. It means easier to process, which is often helpful but not always the goal. Clarity, accuracy and structure matter more than the number.
